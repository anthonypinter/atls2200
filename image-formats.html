The right format for the job

The same photo can be a 60 KB file or a 6 MB file depending on how you save it, and that difference decides whether your page loads in a blink or makes a visitor on a phone wait. Four formats cover nearly everything:

  • JPG for photos. It's lossy: it throws away detail your eye won't miss, which makes files small. Save it too low and you get smudgy blocks.
  • PNG for screenshots and images with sharp edges or see-through backgrounds. It's lossless, so nothing is thrown away, but photos saved as PNG are huge.
  • SVG for logos and icons. It isn't pixels at all, but shapes described in code, so it stays sharp at any size and is often just a few KB.
  • WebP does the jobs of both JPG and PNG, usually in a smaller file. Every modern browser supports it.

The format is only half of it. A photo straight off a phone is often 4000 pixels wide. If it shows 800 pixels wide on your page, you're making every visitor download five times more than they can see. Before uploading:

  1. Resize to about twice the width it appears on screen, so it stays sharp on high-density screens.
  2. Compress it. A free tool like Squoosh lets you compare quality settings side by side.

Aim for under 200 KB for a large photo, and a few KB for icons and logos.

Watch out

Renaming photo.png to photo.jpg changes the name, not the format. To change format, open the image and export it again.

Cheat sheet

Which format, when

JPG .jpg · lossy

Use for photos.

Avoid for logos, text and anything needing a transparent background. JPG has no transparency.

PNG .png · lossless

Use for screenshots, flat graphics with text, and images with transparent backgrounds.

Avoid for photos. They come out 5–10× bigger than a JPG.

WebP .webp · either

Use for almost anything. Usually 25–35% smaller than JPG or PNG at the same quality, and supports transparency.

Note Every current browser supports it. Some older desktop apps can't open it.

AVIF .avif · either

Use for photos when you want the smallest file. Often half the size of a JPG.

Note Slower to export, and not every editor can save it yet. Offer a JPG fallback with <picture>.

SVG .svg · vector

Use for logos, icons and simple illustrations. It's math, not pixels, so it stays sharp at any size and is often tiny.

Avoid for photos. They can't be drawn as shapes.

GIF .gif · old

Use for almost nothing now. Only 256 colors.

Instead for an animation, use a short muted MP4 in a <video>. It's often 10× smaller.

Key ideas

  • Resize before you upload. Export about twice the width the image appears on screen, so it stays sharp on high-density screens. A photo shown 600px wide needs about 1200px, not the 4000px from your phone.
  • Then compress it. Free tools like Squoosh (squoosh.app) and ImageOptim let you compare quality settings side by side. Quality 70–80 is usually indistinguishable from 100.
  • A good target for a large photo is under 200 KB. Most icons and logos should be a few KB.
  • An SVG can go in an <img> like any other image, or be pasted into the HTML directly so CSS can recolor it.

Common mistakes

  • Saving a logo as JPG, which leaves blurry smudges around the letters and a white box instead of transparency.
  • Saving a photo as PNG "for quality," and getting a file several times bigger with no visible difference.
  • Renaming photo.png to photo.jpg. That changes the name, not the format. Re-export it instead.
  • Re-saving the same JPG over and over. Each save loses a little more detail. Keep an original and export copies.

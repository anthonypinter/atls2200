Emphasis and Advanced Text

HTML has an element for almost every kind of text: stressed words, important warnings, quotes, abbreviations, code, dates. Learn which to reach for, and why the meaning matters more than the look.

You'll be able to

  • Use <em> and <strong> for emphasis and importance, and know when <i>, <b> and <u> fit instead.
  • Mark up quotations and their sources with <blockquote>, <q> and <cite>.
  • Mark up abbreviations, contact details, superscripts, subscripts and code.
  • Give dates and times a machine-readable form with <time datetime>.

Emphasis and importance

When you speak, you stress certain words to change what a sentence means. <em> (emphasis) does that in HTML. Browsers show it in italics, and screen readers can say it with a different tone:

<p>I <em>said</em> the trail was closed.</p>

<strong> marks text as important: something the reader mustn't miss. Browsers show it in bold:

<p><strong>Warning:</strong> the bridge washed out in May.</p>

They can nest: <strong>Do <em>not</em> cross the creek.</strong>

Read

Italic, bold, underline…

<i>, <b> and <u> come from the early web, when HTML described looks. Today each has a narrower meaning, for text that's set apart but not stressed or important:

  • <i>: foreign words, scientific names, titles of ships, a thought. <i lang="la">Pinus ponderosa</i>
  • <b>: keywords or product names you want to draw the eye to, without extra importance.
  • <u>: text marked as wrong, like a misspelling. Readers expect underlined text to be a link, so use it rarely.

If you only want the look, use CSS. If none of these meanings fit, you probably want <em> or <strong>.

Quotations

A long quote that stands on its own goes in a <blockquote>; a short one inside a sentence goes in a <q>, which adds quotation marks for you. Both can say where the quote came from with a cite attribute holding a URL:

<blockquote cite="https://www.nps.gov/romo/">
  <p>Take only pictures, leave only footprints.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>The ranger called it <q>the best view on the Front Range</q>.</p>

Browsers don't show the cite attribute anywhere. To show a source, name it on the page with <cite>, which is for the title of a work: a book, an article, a film. <p>From <cite>Colorado Hiking Guide</cite>.</p>

Abbreviations, contact details, superscript and subscript

<abbr> marks an abbreviation. Spell it out in full the first time it appears, or give the full form in a title attribute: <abbr title="Rocky Mountain National Park">RMNP</abbr>. (You may see <acronym> in old code. It's been removed from HTML.)

<address> holds contact details for the page or article it's in, not just any postal address. <sup> and <sub> raise and lower text: the 2 in 14,259 ft2 or in H2O.

Representing computer code

Five elements cover code and computers. <code> for a piece of code; <pre> around it to keep its spacing; <var> for a variable's name; <kbd> for keys the reader presses; <samp> for what a program prints:

<p>Press <kbd>Ctrl</kbd> + <kbd>S</kbd> to save.</p>

Marking up times and dates

  

    
People write dates in many ways: Saturday, June 14, 6/14, 14 June 2026. Computers can't reliably read those. <time> wraps the human version and adds one standard version in its datetime attribute, so calendars, search engines and scripts can understand it:

    
<p>Next group hike: <time datetime="2026-06-14T07:30">Saturday, June 14 at 7:30am</time>.</p>

Key ideas

      
    
        
  • <em> is stress emphasis; <strong> is importance.
    • 
        
  • <i>, <b> and <u> have narrow meanings now. For looks alone, use CSS.
    • 
        
  • <blockquote> for long quotes, <q> for short ones, <cite> for a work's title.
    • 
        
  • <abbr title="…"> expands abbreviations.
    • 
        
  • <time datetime="…"> gives dates a form computers understand.
    • 
      

    

    

      
Common mistakes

      
    
        
  • Using <b> or <i> just to make text bold or italic.
    • 
        
  • Using <blockquote> to indent text that isn't a quote.
    • 
        
  • Putting a person's name in <cite>. It's for the title of a work.
    • 
        
  • Typing quotation marks around a <q>, so they appear twice.
    • 
        
  • A datetime in the wrong format, like datetime="June 14".

Structuring Documents

Most pages share the same parts: a header, navigation, main content, a sidebar and a footer. HTML has an element for each. Use them, and screen reader users can jump straight to the part they want.

  • Know when a <div> or <span> is the right choice, and when it isn't.
  • Plan a small site with a sitemap.

Basic sections of a document

Look at almost any website and you'll find the same parts:

  • Header: a strip across the top with the site's name or logo. It stays the same on every page.
  • Navigation bar: links to the site's main sections, usually in or below the header.
  • Main content: the big area in the middle that's different on every page.
  • Sidebar: extra information beside the main content: related links, ads, an author bio.
  • Footer: a strip across the bottom with small print, contact details and copyright.

HTML for structuring content

Each part has an element that says what it is:

<header>
  <h1>Boulder Trail Club</h1>
</header>
<nav>…links…</nav>
<main>
  <article>
    <h2>Royal Arch Trail</h2>
    <section><h3>Getting there</h3>…</section>
    <section><h3>What to bring</h3>…</section>
  </article>
  <aside><h2>Nearby trails</h2>…</aside>
</main>
<footer>© 2026 Boulder Trail Club</footer>

These elements don't change how the page looks: without CSS, they stack like plain blocks. What they change is what the page means. Screen readers turn them into landmarks that users can jump between, and they make your HTML easier to read and to style.

HTML layout elements in more detail

  • <main>: the content unique to this page. Use it once, directly inside <body>, not inside other parts.
  • <article>: a complete piece that would make sense on its own, like a blog post, a trail write-up or a product card.
  • <section>: a part of a page or article that groups related content, usually with its own heading.
  • <aside>: content related to the main content but not part of it: a sidebar, a glossary box, related links.
  • <header>: introductory content. Directly in <body> it's the site header; inside an <article> it's that article's header.
  • <nav>: a group of main navigation links. Not every group of links needs one.
  • <footer>: closing content for the page, or for the article it's in.

Non-semantic wrappers

Sometimes you need a box to style or to group things, and no element fits. That's what <div> (a block) and <span> (inline, inside text) are for. They mean nothing, so they're safe. Just reach for a semantic element first, and only use a <div> when none fits: a page built only of divs is "div soup".

Line breaks and horizontal rules

<br> breaks a line where the line break is part of the content, like an address or a poem. Don't use it to add space. <hr> marks a thematic break, a change of topic between paragraphs. Browsers draw it as a line, but its job is meaning, not decoration.

    Structuring a basic website

    Before you write a site with several pages, plan it on paper:

    1. List what every page shares: the header, the navigation menu and the footer.
    2. Sketch the layout of a typical page: where the main content, sidebar and footer go.
    3. List everything the site needs to say, then group it into pages.
    4. Draw a sitemap: a diagram of the pages and how they link.

    Breaks that belong

    Use <br> and <hr> for meaning, and remove the ones used for spacing.

    Key ideas

    • Pages share parts: header, nav, main content, sidebar, footer.
    • Each has an element, and screen readers turn them into landmarks.
    • One <main> per page, directly in the body.
    • <article> stands on its own; <section> groups part of something.
    • <div> and <span> are for when nothing else fits.
    • <br> for line breaks that are content; <hr> for a change of topic.

    Common mistakes

    • Building everything from divs with class names like header and nav.
    • More than one <main>, or a <main> inside the header or an article.
    • Using <section> as a styling box with no heading. A div fits better.
    • Stacks of <br>s or an <hr> just to add space or a line.
